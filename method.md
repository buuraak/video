# Method

The steps we follow for every film, the checks we always run, and every mistake that cost us time, so it never happens twice.

## Steps for every film

1. **Ask before building.** Go through the scene description and ask about anything that could go more than one way (camera angle, what a word like "red eyes" means, timing, sound). Don't guess.
   When a choice is about how something looks, or feedback is vague ("looks a bit weird"), draw the options side by side in one picture and let the user pick (it worked for the red eyes, his upper body, the whiteboard and the phone message: `sheets/eye_options.png`, `sheets/body_options.png`, `sheets/board_options.png`, `sheets/message_options.png`). When the choice is about movement, render a short preview video with the options one after another instead (it worked for the reset's snap back: `sheets/reset_options_1-2.6.png` and `_5-6.6.png` show frames from it).
2. **Build the scene.**
3. **Look at it.** Make a contact sheet of the whole scene, look at it yourself and fix what's wrong, then show the user.

## Checks we always run

- The same frame always looks the same. Nothing random may change between runs.
- Any hand or foot that can't reach its target is reported.
- A sound that runs through several scenes (the hum) measures the same loudness in each.

- **ElevenLabs gives a few sounds with long gaps** when we ask for something continuous (marker squeaks, gasping). Then cut the single sounds out (find their peaks) and place one on each action: a squeak every other letter (`scenes/new_goals.py`), a gasp on each breath (`scenes/too_fast.py`). The sound then follows the picture exactly. For a sound that should run on (a pen writing, `scenes/day_one_harder.py`), keep only its loud parts and join them up.

## Mistakes that cost time

- **His running arms read as hands on hips, then as touching his mouth** (film 1, row 11): bent arms with the hands low, then a swing so high the front hand reached his chin. Now: the upper arm swings from the shoulder (about 30 degrees each way, against the legs) and the forearm points forward, so the front hand is at chest height and the back one by his hip (`Gait` in `scenes/too_fast.py`). Then, a third time, the user spotted T-rex arms: the elbow was worked out low and behind, but the arm was told to bend the other way, so the elbow flipped out in front. Now: an arm or leg can reach the same point bending two ways; when we decide where the elbow or knee goes, pick the bend that puts it there. And a sprinter leans into it, and his heel kicks up behind him before the leg comes through (it doesn't drag along the ground). A fourth time, the user saw his back arm stuck: both arms only swung in front of him, and the swing was cut off at each end, so the back hand sat still by his hip for part of every stride. Now: each arm follows the stride smoothly (from his steps' rhythm, not clamped), from behind his hip to chest height in front. When checking a movement, trace each limb's position over time, not just a few frames.

For each one: what happened, what it cost, and what we do now to avoid it.

- **Hand and foot targets guessed in screen pixels.** Three times while building the toolkit, targets were out of reach and the pose had to be redone. Now: place targets relative to the body (`character.joints(pose)["shoulder"]` or `["hip"]`), and never ignore a "can't reach" warning. It happened again in film 1's night scene: a spot on the phone, placed relative to the phone, was 20 px out of reach. When a target sits on an object near the edge of his reach, measure its distance from his shoulder against his arm (198 px at bedroom size). In row 12 his breathing lifted his hips above where the walk put them, and a foot was 1 px out of reach: breathing on top of a walk only ever lowers the hips.
- **The board scene was way too fast** (film 1, 6 s): the scene table gave it 6 seconds, and five arm moves plus a walk were squeezed into them. Rebuilt at 10 s. Now: before building, list every action with its time. A tired character needs most of a second per move, and on-screen writing needs a moment to be read. If the scene table's time can't hold that, say so and agree a new length before building. Since film 1's row 10 ("everything goes by way too fast"), the rule is: plan every beat generously and don't squeeze scenes into the table's times; the table follows the scenes, not the other way round. In row 12, retimed beats got squeezed without anyone deciding it (his sag started on the cut, so it never showed; a marker had 0.05 s to get back to the tray): set each moment from the one before it (`CLOSE_UP = SAG[1] + 0.3`), so moving one beat moves the rest.
- **In a close-up, his arm came in from the wrong side** (film 1, the board): in the wide shot he stood left of the board, but in the close-up his arm rose from the bottom right. Caught on the contact sheet before showing it. Now: a limb in a close-up comes from the side the character stands on in the shot before.
- **A close-up arm's elbow went off the top of the picture** (film 1, clock shot), twice: it looked fine resting, but rose out of frame when the arm lifted. Now: check the highest point of a moving limb across its whole move, and make a contact sheet of just that stretch (`--start`/`--end`). It happened again in row 9's slam (film 1): measuring the elbow's highest point across the move caught it, and lowering the off-screen shoulder fixed it.
- **Curved lines around a head read as cartoon "shaking" lines or a halo** (film 1, pillow creases), twice. Now: don't draw arcs around a character's head unless they're meant to show movement.
- **Seen from above, his arm crossed his face** (film 1, scene 2), three times: tilting his body to reach, an elbow bending upward, and the phone's path passing over his head. Now: in overhead shots, bend elbows down toward the bed and route his hands and anything he carries below his head. If an elbow has to switch sides, ease it over while the arm is almost straight, or it jumps.
- **His elbow snapped to the other side of his body in one frame** (film 1, scene 2, as he held the phone up). One rule placed the elbow while he reached and another while he held the phone, and the first simply switched off. Nobody noticed it in the 17-second version; it showed up when the scene was shortened. Now: when one placement of a joint hands over to another, blend from one into the other. Start the next move from where the body really is: in row 10 a foot was still landing from his last step when he knelt, and dropped to the floor in one frame. After changing a move, track how far each joint moves between frames: any jump far bigger than its neighbours is a snap. A related one (film 1, night): his hand paused at a low point while the elbow switched sides, so the elbow swung down and back up on its own. Now: move the hand through such a point in one smooth curve, and spread the switch over about half a second around the moment it passes. And a hand that passes close under its own shoulder makes the elbow whip round in a frame or two (film 1, row 9, throwing the blanket): route the hand farther from the shoulder.
- **The dark eyelid patches showed as black bars once light fell on his face** (film 1, scene 2). Now: draw the face first, then add light over it.
- **A scene made no sense to the viewer** (film 1, row 10, first version): he walked in already wearing the shoes he then knelt to tie, and went out of the door with nothing telling us what he was going to do. Now: before building, read the beats as a viewer who only sees the film: do things appear when they should, and do we know what he's doing and why?
- **His hands at his feet didn't read from the side** (film 1, row 10, tying his shoes): his arms are a third of his height, so to reach his shoes he has to fold right over, and his arms tangle with his legs. Now: show hand work near his feet in a close-up (row 10 cuts to his shoe).
- **He walked hunched over, twice** (film 1, row 10). First his hips were lowered to keep long strides in reach; then, with that undone, he still looked hunched, because his hips sat low the whole time (both knees always bent, like sneaking) and his arms hung bent in front of his hips. Compared side by side in `sheets/walk_compare.png`. Now: the leg he stands on is almost straight, so his hips ride up over it and dip a little between steps (smoothed, never out of reach); his arms hang nearly straight from the shoulder and swing against his legs; his feet land midway along their stride, and he speeds up from standing. `Walk` in `scenes/new_goals.py` does all of this: use it for every walk from the side.
- **Walking away from us looked like he'd soiled himself** (film 1, row 29): drawn with the front-view figure, his feet stood wide apart and his knees bowed outward as they stepped. Now: a figure seen from behind or in front walks with its feet close together and its legs as straight lines to the feet (a leg stepping away just looks shorter); place everything in depth and shrink it with distance (`project()` in `scenes/out.py`). And through a door or window, always show a real place, not a flat colour.
- **His arms and legs jumped in a sped-up shot** (film 1, row 23, cleaning the room): moving at time-lapse speed, his limbs swung as fast as his walking and stopped dead at each stop, so they leapt up to 300 px in a frame. Now: in a sped-up walk his steps swing slower than he moves, their swing follows how fast he's really going (smoothed), and he turns while he stops; track the biggest joint move per frame (under about 100 px).
- **A prop vanished between scenes** (film 1): the eraser lay on the board's tray in row 10, but rows 12-20 drew the tray without it; caught only when row 21 needed it, and seven scenes had to be rendered again. Now: when a scene leaves something in a shared place, put it in the shared piece every later scene uses (`BoardArm.on_tray` now includes the eraser), and look for it on the contact sheet.
- **A sound carried over from the scene before started in a silence** (film 1, row 9): the alarm rings on from the reset, but this scene began in the gap between two bursts of beeps, so he would have slammed a silent clock. Caught on the contact sheet. Now: when a scene picks up a repeating sound, work out where in its rhythm it starts (`alarm_on()`), and time the action to it.
- **The discipline bar changed without anyone noticing** (film 1): it filled and flickered silently up in the black bar while the eye was on him, and every scene had to be rendered again. Now: anything that changes away from where the viewer is looking gets its own sound.
- **A scene's soundtrack nearly clipped** (film 1, row 13): set so the hum matched, its `PEAK_DB` came out above 0, which would have distorted its loudest moment. Now: if `PEAK_DB` comes out above about -1, turn its loudest sounds down instead.
- **The hum got louder from scene 1 to scene 2**, because each scene's sound is turned up until its loudest moment hits the top, and scene 2's loudest sound is quieter than scene 1's alarm. Now: set the scene's `PEAK_DB` so its whole soundtrack is turned up by +2.8 dB, like every scene so far (compare the mix's loudest moment with `PEAK_DB`). Measuring a stretch of the hum itself doesn't work: it swells and fades, so different moments give readings up to 4 dB apart.
- **The hero floated a few pixels above the floor** (you spotted it in the demo). His resting feet moved with his breathing hips, and the hand-drawn floor line drifted up to 4 px every 3 frames. Now: `stand()` keeps feet planted on the floor, and floors are drawn with `ink.floor(y)`, which never drifts. Checks guard both.
